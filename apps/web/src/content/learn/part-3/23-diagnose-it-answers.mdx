Keep your notes and trace open as you compare these answers with the ones
you wrote in
[Diagnosing a request from its trace](/learn/part-3/22-diagnose-it).
If you haven't tried the exercise yet, return to it first. You'll get more
from this comparison once you've had a chance to form your own explanation.

To orient ourselves, the mixed scenario runs four rules on one `expense` event:

- A and B post to `/ok`.
- C posts to `/slow?ms=15000`, past the 10 second run timeout.
- D posts to `/fail?status=500`.

Don't worry if your trace ids or exact timings differ. We're looking for
the same outcomes, relative durations and overlapping spans, rather than
an exact match down to the millisecond.

## The slowest operation

Start with the longest `Engine.execute` span. It belongs to rule C and
lasts about 10 seconds.
The receiver was set to respond after 15 seconds, but the run's timeout
interrupts the POST before that response arrives. Because the timeout is
inside the execution span, the span includes the full wait and ends with
`TimeoutError`.

Now check that duration against the recorded result. C's span has status
Error, and its row in `runs list` is
`failed (timeout): timed out after 10s`. The duration and recorded reason
explain why the run ended at about 10 seconds rather than waiting all 15.

## The failed operation

For D, the error details tell a different story. It received a 500
response. Its `Engine.execute` span has status
Error with the message
`POST http://localhost:4600/fail?status=500 answered 500`. Its run row
shows `failed (status)`, with the 500 in the error details.

C also failed. If you named both C and D and distinguished their reasons,
your answer is correct. D received a response outside the accepted
2xx range; C did not receive a response before its timeout.

## The work that completed

A and B are the successful runs. You can confirm that in both places:
their run rows show `succeeded`, and their execution spans have status Ok.

To see whether they waited, compare the start times. All four
`Engine.execute` spans start close together
under the same `Engine.handleEvent` parent. With concurrency set to 4,
these four rules can all start without waiting for another to finish. A,
B and D finish long before C, so C's slow request does not delay their
individual outcomes.

This doesn't mean the CLI returns as soon as A and B finish. The event
request still waits for all four results before returning. It
takes about 10 seconds, determined by C's timeout rather than by the sum of
the run durations. You can confirm this in the `Engine.handleEvent`
duration and in the elapsed time of `events submit`.

## Evidence of the timeout

There are two places to confirm that C reached the timeout. Its execution
span lasts about 10 seconds and
ends with `TimeoutError`, while its run row records the reason `timeout`.
These are two separate records of the same outcome. If you relied only
on the duration, add the error or reason to your notes. A request could
finish in about 10 seconds without timing out; the recorded error and
reason tell us why this one stopped.

## Check the complete result

Bring those observations together. You should find four run rows and four
execution spans, matching the previous lesson's checkpoint. Two spans are
red: C for the timeout and D
for the 500 response. The other two have status Ok. Together, the rows
explain the outcomes and the overlapping spans show that the rules ran
concurrently.

You've followed an expense through several independent actions and
explained both the results and the time spent waiting. The history tells
us what reflex recorded; the trace shows how the work overlapped. Keeping
both is what lets us make that explanation after the request has ended.
