Tim asked Kit not to send us 100 pull requests a day.

Kit gave his agent a goal of 99.

We ended up with **207 PRs, 195 merges, a maxed-out GitHub runner quota, and a Slack channel full of people actively making the situation worse.** I was one of those people. This is my account of the incident.

The participants were [Kit Langton](https://github.com/kitlangton) from [Anomaly](https://anomaly.co/), OpenAI's Astra, and the [Effectful](https://effectful.co/) team. On our side, [Tim Smart](https://github.com/tim-smart) had his own AI infrastructure, built with [Multica](https://multica.ai/). We call it **The Boys**.

Kit orchestrated the bug hunt. The Boys helped us work through the incoming PRs. The humans gave the final feedback and decided what to merge. [Namespace](https://namespace.so/) eventually had to come and rescue our CI.

All of this started with someone being polite.

## It started with one bug. Obviously.

On September 2, Kit dropped into our shared Effectful and Anomaly channel:

> Sorry for PR spamming!

He'd found [a Queue bug](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7576) while working on [OpenCode](https://opencode.ai/). A producer that had been waiting for space could resume and deliver a message more than once. That's the sort of thing you would quite reasonably prefer your queue not to do.

After finding it, he'd sent some agents looking for similar bugs in Effect. They'd come back with more. The findings seemed plausible, he said, but we should feel free to ignore or close the PRs.

Tim's response was immediate in spirit, if not in wall-clock time:

> I shall take your tokens.

Kit described them as "some spare free tokens." Free from his point of view, anyway. Was there any part of the codebase we'd like him to go through with "an unreasonable number of agents"?

This is a dangerous question to ask people who maintain a TypeScript library for a living. We have a very long list of things we would like somebody else to investigate.

Tim told him to go for it. Use the `audit` PR label so he could work through the submissions in bulk. One small request, though. Sebastian Lorenz had previously sent him something like 100 audit PRs a day.

> Maybe don't do that

A sensible boundary. Clearly communicated. What could go wrong?

## How the hell do you review 200 PRs?

This is the part that gets lost if you only look at the number of submissions. Kit had agents producing work. **We also had agents working through it.**

The Boys are Tim's setup built with Multica, which lets him assign work to agents and track it as tasks. A PR can become a job to investigate, with progress and a result to review. Several of those jobs can be in flight while Tim looks at the work that has come back.

The `audit` label mattered for that reason. It made the incoming batch easy to find and work through. The joke about Tim's calloused hands was attached to a real requirement for keeping the queue organized.

Here's the board Tim shared while Kit was still arguing that thirty was less than a hundred:

  caption="The Boys at work. Several investigations in progress, several results ready for review."

You can see individual PRs represented as work items, with fixes in progress and others waiting for review. That is how the incoming flood became work we could handle. Kit didn't have to wait for Tim to finish one investigation before another could start, and Tim didn't have to personally do every mechanical step of each fix.

The agents could investigate the reported behavior, work on a reproduction, revise the implementation, and bring back code and test results. Tim then had something concrete to judge. His feedback sent the work around again when the implementation was wrong, the test was in the wrong place, or the proposed API wasn't one we wanted to maintain.

**The final human feedback was the important bit.** An agent saying "done" meant there was work to inspect. It didn't mean the PR had earned a merge.

For this batch, the feedback included very ordinary maintainer decisions. Use the existing test suite. Match the overload above. Keep one or two tests that demonstrate the regression. Preserve the old API and introduce a separate one. Or, occasionally, delete the whole function.

The Boys could do the follow-through. The humans decided what the follow-through should be.

### Tim would like one fewer function

Take [#7657](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7657). The finding was that `Channel.runDone` returned the wrong completion value. A perfectly reasonable thing for a bug-finding agent to bring us: here's a function, here's what it promises, here's what it actually does.

Tim looked at the proposed fix and asked a different question.

<Backchannel
  source="Human feedback · GitHub"
  date="PR #7657"
  messages={[
    {
      author: "Tim Smart",
      text: "Lets just remove it as it does the same thing as runDrain?",
      href: "https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7657#discussion_r3919950524",

/>

The resulting PR removed `runDone` and updated the tests, docs, migration guidance, and changeset to use `runDrain`.

A bug finder had arrived with a broken function. A maintainer decided we didn't need the function. That still counts as a merged PR, but it tells you considerably more about the process than a green checkmark does.

The same thing happened with [#7987](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7987). The original proposal narrowed `Multipart.isPart` to streamed parts. Tim wanted to preserve the existing broad guard and add `isStreamPart` for the narrower check. His [review comment](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7987#discussion_r3930780970) became the final design. The follow-up changed the implementation, tests, documentation, and even the PR wording to match.

This is what we mean by human feedback. You can ask an agent to make a patch satisfy a test. Somebody still has to decide whether that patch is the API you want people to use next year.

### Ninety tests walk into a repository

The other recurring conversation was about tests. Agents are very willing to write tests. Getting them to stop writing tests can be its own project.

In [#8045](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/8045), a line-ending fix for migration-document tooling arrived with a standalone 90-case matrix. Tim reduced it to three cases in the existing suite.

Ninety. For line endings. Somewhere a carriage return was receiving more individual attention than most of our public APIs.

The review comments elsewhere in the batch tell the same story. On [#7945](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7945), Tim asked for one or two tests that prevent the regression. On [#7829](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7829), he pointed out that the test was in the wrong file. It moved into the real libSQL integration suite, and the standalone file disappeared.

That is useful agent work followed by useful human feedback followed by more useful agent work. The fix gets tested, and we avoid adopting a small private testing framework every time somebody finds a bug.

When we checked the GitHub histories, **185 of the 195 merged PRs retained maintainer-authored commits**. Tim appears in 184 of them; Giulio in one. There were recorded force pushes on 102 merged branches. A lot happened between "here is a bug" and "this belongs on main."

Those author names don't tell you which lines somebody typed by hand. The Boys' work also runs through Tim's account, and branches were rewritten during review. They do show why the merge rate is a measure of the whole process, including our revisions, rather than a grade for Astra's first attempt.

## And yes, the findings were good

For all the jokes about slop, Kit was sending us things worth fixing. This was a broad audit of Effect v4, with findings across the runtime, data structures, platform integrations, SQL, type declarations, and tooling.

Some examples of what was coming through the queue:

- **Old cleanup touching new state.** Several cache and resource fixes dealt with an interrupted or stale operation removing a newer entry. [#7585](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7585) protects newer cache entries when a refresh is interrupted. [#7586](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7586) handles ownership when an old `RcRef` borrower releases a resource. These are the bugs where each operation looks reasonable on its own and the ordering ruins your afternoon.
- **Data disappearing from collections.** [#7617](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7617) fixes concatenation of sliced chunks. [#7774](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7774) stops removing a Trie child from also pruning a prefix that still has a value. Deleting `"abc"` should not make `"ab"` disappear as a bonus.
- **HTTP cleanup and wire formats.** [#7679](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7679) closes request scopes for streaming HEAD responses, where there is no response body to finish the cleanup. [#7683](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7683) preserves valid JSON-RPC control IDs of `0` and `""`. Falsy is not the same thing as missing, however enthusiastically JavaScript encourages the confusion.
- **SQL behaving differently on reuse.** [#7635](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7635) fixes placeholder numbering in cached returning fragments. [#7829](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7829) isolates transaction contexts between libSQL clients. Both are considerably more interesting than a spelling correction.
- **AI integrations mangling payloads.** [#7756](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7756) and [#7758](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7758) address base64 image handling in the OpenAI and Anthropic integrations. Double-encoding an image is a creative way to ensure nobody sees it.
- **Types and tools disagreeing with the code.** The run also found missing service requirements, recovery signatures accepting the wrong types, generated clients encoding forms as JSON, and Markdown/MDX examples that weren't getting executed properly. The audit eventually reached the tools we use to maintain the library itself.

Some changes needed more work after they landed. Tim's [PubSub follow-up](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7607) arrived less than twelve minutes after [the original merge](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7603), fixing a new polling check that confused a sentinel published as real data with an empty subscription. Giulio consolidated JSON Pointer and array-index handling into shared helpers in [#7823](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7823) and [#7855](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7855).

The work kept moving after the merge button. That's maintenance. The funny part was how much of it had shown up because Kit decided to spend those tokens on Effect.

## Works on main. Case closed.

The runner migration also gave us a good example of why the humans were still in the conversation.

Sebastian reported that [#7768](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect/pull/7768), a generated form-urlencoded request fix, broke on Bun. Tim sent an investigation through The Boys and shared the "Team Lead" report. The targeted suite passed all 13 tests, including all six request-encoding variants. No code change or PR was needed, the report concluded.

Sebastian replied:

> It does break ... Or rather, it did before I merged this:

He linked the runner PR.

The agent had tested `main` at `4372c79a32`. Sebastian's repair was already in that revision. He'd replaced the new test helper's Node/Babel compilation with Rolldown while expanding Bun test coverage.

So the tests passed. The agent was correct about that. It had successfully verified the fix Sebastian had already made, then delivered the excellent news that nobody needed to fix it.

The code change was in the test helper and CI execution. The human contribution to the discussion was remembering what had happened earlier that morning.

This is why a report comes back to a person. Somebody has to connect the result to the question we were actually asking.

It also needed an off switch.

Both parts matter. Astra found bugs we wanted fixed. Tim and The Boys worked through the patches, and the humans kept making decisions about the code we would have to live with afterward.
